4. PAdES Cryptographic Architecture & ByteRange Hashing

To understand how digital signatures achieve tamper-evidence, one must look at the PAdES (PDF Advanced Electronic Signatures - ETSI EN 319 142) and ISO 32000-1 §12.8 signature dictionary architecture.

In a digitally signed PDF, the document contains a signature dictionary with a critical key: /ByteRange. 

/ByteRange [0 84210 96500 42100]

This array of four integers specifies two byte ranges:

  • Range 1 (0 84210): From the beginning of the file (byte 0) up to the exact point where the cryptographic signature hex stream begins (byte 84,210).
  • Range 2 (96500 42100): From the end of the cryptographic signature hex stream (byte 96,500) to the very end of the file.

The gap between byte 84,210 and 96,500 is where the PKCS#7 cryptographic envelope resides. A SHA-256 hash is computed over all the bytes in Range 1 and Range 2. If a bad actor modifies a single character, punctuation mark, or dollar amount anywhere in the document, the newly computed hash will mismatch the signed hash stored in the envelope, instantly flagging the document as tampered and invalid in Adobe Acrobat and PDF readers.

9. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Are electronic signatures legally binding under federal and international law?

Yes. Under the United States Federal ESIGN Act (15 U.S.C. § 7001), Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA), and European Union eIDAS Regulation (No 910/2014, Article 25(1)), an electronic signature cannot be denied legal validity solely because it is in digital form. Simple Electronic Signatures (SES) are valid for 99% of commercial agreements, including NDAs, employment contracts, leases, and invoices.

What is the difference between a Simple Electronic Signature (SES) and a Digital Signature (QES)?

An electronic signature (SES) is an electronic mark or process attached to a document demonstrating intent to sign. A digital signature (QES) utilizes public key infrastructure (PKI) cryptographic envelopes with X.509 certificates and hardware tokens. QES is strictly mandated for specific statutory instruments like wills and real estate title deeds, whereas SES covers everyday business agreements.

Why is Classic Navy Blue ink recommended over pure black for legal contracts?

In commercial and court practices, blue ink (#003366 or #0284C7) is strongly preferred over black ink. Blue ink immediately distinguishes an authentic original execution mark from black-and-white photocopies, establishing clear prima facie visual authenticity.

How does LocalDoc remove paper backgrounds from uploaded signature photos?

LocalDoc's image-processing engine runs client-side luminance thresholding and alpha-channel isolation in browser RAM. It analyzes background paper brightness, isolates dark ink strokes, and makes the surrounding paper pixels completely transparent (Alpha 0.0), ensuring zero opaque white boxes cover contract text.

Can I sign password-protected or encrypted PDF documents?

If a PDF has an Owner Password restricting editing, LocalDoc cannot inject signatures until authorized. Users can open LocalDoc's Protect & Unlock tool to decrypt the file in browser RAM using their valid password, sign the document, and optionally re-encrypt it prior to emailing.

Does LocalDoc store copies of my signed contracts or signatures?

No. LocalDoc operates under a strict zero-upload architecture. All file parsing, signature drawing, vector spline compilation, and PDF rendering occur in volatile browser memory on your local machine. No contracts or signature graphics are ever transmitted to cloud servers.
